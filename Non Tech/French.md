# French

## Learning sequence

1. Greetings and introducing yourself.
2. French sounds, the alphabet, and spelling your name.
3. Subject pronouns and *être* (to be): describing yourself.
4. Nouns, gender, and articles: naming things around you.
5. Numbers and *avoir* (to have): age and possessions.
6. Common present-tense verbs: everyday actions, questions, and negatives.

## Greetings

### Essential expressions

| French | Meaning | Approximate pronunciation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bonjour ! | Hello! / Good morning! | bo(n)-zhoor |
| Je m'appelle… | My name is… | zhuh mah-pell… |
| Et vous ? | And you? | ay voo |
| Merci. | Thank you. | mehr-see |
| Au revoir ! | Goodbye! | oh ruh-vwahr |
| À bientôt ! | See you soon! | ah byeh(n)-toh |

### Saying your name

Use the pattern **Je m'appelle + your name**.

**Je m'appelle Anupreet.** → My name is Anupreet.

**Je** means “I.” **M'appelle** comes from *s'appeler* (to be called), so the natural English translation of the whole expression is “My name is…” Learn **je m'appelle** as a complete phrase for now; we will examine its grammar when we study reflexive verbs.

Keep the apostrophe: **m'appelle** is written as one unit, with no space after the apostrophe.

### Inviting a response from someone

After saying your name, add **Et vous ?** The context makes this mean “And what is your name?”

**Vous** is the polite form of “you” for one person, and it is also used when addressing several people. Here, we use it to speak politely to someone we have just met.

**Et toi ?** (approximately **ay twah**) also means “And you?”, but is informal. Use it when speaking to **one person**, such as a friend, family member, or child.

**Je m'appelle Anupreet. Et toi ?** → My name is Anupreet. And you?

## Accents

French uses five main written marks, commonly taught together as accents. They can guide pronunciation, distinguish words, or preserve older spellings. They are part of the spelling and remain on capital letters: **é → É**, **à → À**.

### Acute accent: accent aigu — é

The acute accent appears only on **e**, producing **é**. It indicates the vowel sound **/e/**: roughly English “ay,” but with a steady vowel rather than a glide at the end.

**Été** means “summer.” Both instances of **é** have this sound.

### Grave accent: accent grave — à, è, ù

Its use depends on the letter:

- **è** indicates **/ɛ/**, an “eh” sound like the vowel in English “bed.” Example: **mère** (mother).
- **à** distinguishes words without changing the pronunciation of **a**: **a** means “has,” while **à** commonly means “to” or “at.”
- **ù** distinguishes **où** (where) from **ou** (or). These two words are pronounced the same.

**Il a un livre à la maison.** → He has a book at home. Here, **a** expresses possession, while **à** introduces the location.

### Circumflex: accent circonflexe — â, ê, î, ô, û

The circumflex can affect pronunciation, distinguish words, or reflect a word's history:

- **Pronunciation:** **ê** has the “eh” sound in **être** (to be). **ô** usually has a closed “o” sound, as in **bientôt** (soon). Pronunciation distinctions involving the circumflex vary by region and word.
- **Meaning:** **sur** means “on”; **sûr** means “sure.” These are normally pronounced the same.
- **History:** It sometimes marks a letter that disappeared from an older spelling, often **s**. The relationship between **forêt** (forest) and English “forest” makes this visible.

Learn the circumflex with the word; it does not simply mean “stress this vowel.”

### Diaeresis: tréma — ë, ï, ü

The two dots usually indicate that adjacent vowels should be pronounced separately, instead of being read as the usual vowel combination.

- **Noël** (Christmas): roughly **no-ell**, with separate **o** and **e** vowel sounds.
- **Naïf** (naive): roughly **nah-eef**, with separate **a** and **i** vowel sounds.

The tréma does not give every vowel the same sound; it tells you how to separate the sounds in the word.

### Cedilla: cédille — ç

The cedilla is the mark **under c**. It gives **c** an **s** sound before **a**, **o**, or **u**, where it would normally have a **k** sound.

- **Français** (French): **ç** before **a**.
- **Garçon** (boy): **ç** before **o**.
- **Reçu** (received): **ç** before **u**.

Before **e** or **i**, **c** already normally has an **s** sound, so no cedilla is needed: **merci** (thank you).
